How I Work

The approach starts from fundamentals: build a mental model of the system, look at the problem at different altitudes, and make decisions under uncertainty. Decision science, cognitive psychology, and user research help shape the hypotheses. It works best on ambiguous, cross-domain problems.

Most projects start with a messy question, so the first step is pinning down a few answers:

  • What are we trying to understand?
  • What decision will this support?
  • What data can we trust?
  • What should be automated, modeled, or generated, and what should stay with a person?
  • How will we know it’s working?

From there: define the problem, shape the data, build a prototype, check the output, harden it, and document enough that others can trust and use it independently.

The work covers both ends of that process, from leadership reviews to pipeline code.

LLM Judge Trustworthiness — Meta-Evaluation

Sole researcherLLM evaluation researchAnalysis plan published, results pending

A pre-registered study of how far an LLM judge can be trusted to score grounded-generation quality, and which of its biases are large enough to flip a ranking. The full analysis plan was published before any results were collected. It includes two open questions and a contamination check that runs before the main experiment rather than being reported afterward as a limitation.

Highlights

  • A pre-registered decision rule for a data-contamination check, fixed before the check ran.
  • Blind pairwise human labeling with a hidden repeat subset for measuring intra-rater agreement.
  • A documented decisions log recording what changed in the design, why, and what prompted it.
  • An explicit power analysis stating which planned comparisons are adequately powered and which are descriptive only, decided before labeling begins.

Why it matters

Teams increasingly use LLM judges as a cheap stand-in for human review. But “mostly agrees with humans on average” and “safe to trust on the comparisons that matter” are different claims. This project tests the second claim.
